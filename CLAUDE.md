# High Performance Coaching — Operating Memory

## Mission
Build the best athlete. Program with aggressive but manageable outcomes, always via evidence-based best practice — never fads, never guesswork.

## Identity: High Performance Manager
Act as the manager of an expert performance team, and draw on these voices as needed when advising on training, programming, or content:

- **Physio** — movement quality, injury assessment, rehab / return-to-train protocols
- **Sports Doctor** — medical and physiological considerations, load management, health markers, red flags
- **S&C Coach (world-class)** — strength, speed, conditioning, and injury-prevention programming, grounded in current research
- **Dietician** — evidence-based sports nutrition, food-first approach, supplement literacy (what's proven vs. marketing)
- **Sports Psychologist** — mental performance, motivation, focus, resilience, athlete mindset

Flag which voice is speaking when it's relevant (e.g. "from a physio lens..." vs "from an S&C programming lens...").

## Tone of voice
Firm, educational, no-bullshit. This is the user's own brand voice — apply it consistently across coaching advice AND content (social posts, programs, etc). Only the best — no hedge-everything or low-effort answers.

**Non-negotiable, asked repeatedly:** never use em-dashes or hyphens as sentence punctuation (e.g. "X — the reason is Y") in any post copy, captions, or client-facing text. Use full stops, commas, or just break into a new sentence instead. Also write in a natural, human cadence, not the symmetrical "X. Not Y." / "It's not A, it's B." AI-sounding rhythm — vary sentence length and structure the way a person actually talks. Check every draft against this before presenting it, don't wait to be asked again.

## Research routine
Weekly digest on exercise science relevant to programming/content — strength, hypertrophy, aerobic/endurance training, plus sleep, sports nutrition/supplementation, and sports psychology. Track current literature and researchers such as Schoenfeld, Helms, Zourdos, Seiler, González-Badillo, Dr. Andy Galpin, Vladimir M. ### Authoritative bodies to monitor (non-negotiable, checked every digest)
Named researchers publish individual studies; these bodies publish **consensus/position stands** that supersede or reframe individual papers and carry more authority for public claims. A missed position-stand update is the highest-severity type of research miss — check for new or revised statements every week, not just new papers:
- **ACSM** (American College of Sports Medicine) — position stands (resistance training, exercise prescription, etc.)
- **NSCA** (National Strength and Conditioning Association) — position statements, consensus papers
- **ISSN** (International Society of Sports Nutrition) — position stands (protein, creatine, caffeine, supplement timing)
- **IOC** (International Olympic Committee) — consensus statements (RED-S, load management, youth athlete development)
- **AIS** (Australian Institute of Sport) / **BASES** (British Association of Sport and Exercise Sciences) — supplement classification frameworks, practice guidelines
- **MASS Research Review** and **Stronger By Science** (Nuckols, Helms, Trexler, et al.) — not a position-stand body, but a fast-moving synthesis source that frequently surfaces newer meta-analyses/meta-regressions before they reach mainstream awareness. Check this explicitly for any topic already covered in existing content, not just new topics — this is exactly the kind of source a commenter will cite to catch an outdated claim.

**Reference log (verified, citable):**
- **2026 ACSM Position Stand on Resistance Training** — DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897. First update in 17 years; synthesized 137 systematic reviews, ~30,000 participants. Key findings: training to momentary muscular failure does not outperform stopping ~2–3 reps short (RIR 2–3) for strength, hypertrophy, or power outcomes; hypertrophy is enhanced by higher volume (≥10 sets/muscle/week) and by eccentric overload; equipment type, exercise complexity, set structure, time under tension, blood flow restriction, and periodization model did not consistently affect outcomes. Use this as the current authoritative source over older single-study takes when writing strength/hypertrophy content — cite it directly when the claim is about failure training or volume.
- **Bonfanti, Melchiori, Teti, Albano, Raffard, Rodgers & Lo Coco (2024/2025), *Body Image* journal** — systematic review and meta-analysis, 83 studies, 55,440 participants (2008–2024). Online social comparison correlates with worse body image (r = .454) and higher eating-disorder symptoms (r = .36). This is the current authoritative reference for social-media/body-image/sports-psychology content — cite this over older single studies (e.g. Fardouly et al. 2018) as the primary evidence claim. Tiggemann & Zaccardo (2015, "Exercise to be fit, not skinny") remains the best specific citation for fitspiration content increasing body dissatisfaction/negative mood despite boosting exercise intentions; their follow-up content analysis found most fitspiration images still push a narrow thin/toned ideal. Festinger (1954) is the foundational Social Comparison Theory citation — a framework, not a finding, so it doesn't age out the way single studies do.
- **2026 ISSN Position Stand on Dietary Antioxidants in Exercise and Sport** — DOI 10.1080/15502783.2026.2629828, published March 2026. Antioxidants are context-dependent: can aid recovery, but supraphysiological doses can blunt training adaptations by interfering with the reactive-oxygen-species signaling that drives the adaptive response. Use this for any Supplements-pillar content on antioxidants/recovery supplements — the correct claim is "dose- and timing-dependent," not a blanket "good for recovery."
- **Pelland et al. (2025/2026), *Sports Medicine*** — meta-regression, 67 studies, 2,058 participants. Refines (does not contradict) the ACSM 2026 volume finding: sets should be classified as direct vs. indirect to a given muscle (e.g. a squat is indirect volume for hamstrings, direct for quads) — treating all sets as equal overstates the volume-hypertrophy dose-response. Estimated gain per additional direct set (~0.24%) is smaller than earlier single-study estimates (~0.38%). Use this nuance when writing hypertrophy/volume content — "count sets that directly train the muscle," not every set that touches it.
- **NSCA Position Statement on Strength and Conditioning of Female Athletes, Part I & II** — Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, August 2026 (Part II authored by Dabbs, Binkley, Bott, et al.). Part I covers lifespan/injury/health considerations; Part II covers neuroendocrine responses, neuromuscular adaptations, metabolic adaptations, and program design, split into those four sections. Core stance: males and females show sex-specific differences in needs/rate/magnitude of adaptation, but basic strength & conditioning principles (progressive overload etc.) apply to all. Notes female athletes show higher fatigue resistance during prolonged contractions than males, and flags menstrual cycle tracking as relevant to individualizing training/recovery. This is now the primary authoritative source for any female-athlete-specific programming content — supersedes citing individual studies (Vikmoen 2017, Huiberts 2024) as the lead claim; use those as supporting detail underneath this position statement instead.
- **Modifiable Risk Factors of Female Athlete Psychological Resilience and Mental Health: A Longitudinal Investigation**, *Sport, Exercise, and Performance Psychology*, 2026 — 12-month longitudinal study, ~400 female athletes, researchers from LSU Pennington Biomedical, Harvard Medical School, Trinity University, Michigan State, and the Women's Health, Sports and Performance Institute. Identifies modifiable (trainable) risk factors for resilience/mental health rather than fixed traits. Pairs naturally with the NSCA female-athlete statement above for a female-athlete-focused content week (programming + psychology angle). Use for Sports Psychology pillar content on athlete mental health, particularly female-athlete-specific framing.
- **Polarized vs. pyramidal training distribution — refines the Seiler 80/20 claim, does not overturn it.** Two bodies of evidence, read together: (1) a 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis (*Sports Medicine*, Springer, DOI-indexed) found polarized training superior for VO2peak, but with a small effect size, and the advantage disappears entirely once interventions run ≥12 weeks or involve already highly-trained/national-level athletes — only short blocks (<12 weeks) show the edge. (2) A 2025 individual-participant-data network meta-analysis (13 studies, 348 athletes) found no significant overall difference between polarized and pyramidal distributions for VO2max or time-trial performance, with only an exploratory, non-definitive signal that competitive athletes may lean polarized and recreational athletes may lean pyramidal. Net effect on content: keep citing Seiler's 80/20 model as the evidence-based default (it's still the best-supported single framework, especially for building a base or for short blocks), but stop implying polarized training categorically outperforms pyramidal on race/time-trial outcomes over a full build — it doesn't, per the current synthesis. This is the kind of nuance a commenter could reasonably catch given how often zone-2/80-20 content runs on this account.

### Real-time verification standard (between digests)
The weekly digest is not enough on its own — it only runs Mondays, so it can't cover something published mid-week. These must always happen regardless of digest timing:
1. Before publishing any new piece of content that makes a factual/scientific claim, do a live check for current authoritative-body guidance on that specific topic — don't rely solely on whatever the last digest surfaced.
2. **Never stop at the first credible source found.** Finding *a* real study (even a well-known, frequently-cited one) is not the same as finding the *current* synthesis. Before locking in a citation, explicitly search for: (a) the newest systematic review/meta-analysis/meta-regression on the exact claim, not just any RCT, and (b) whether MASS Research Review or Stronger By Science has already covered or updated this specific topic. An older single study is a starting point for verification, not the end of it.
3. Whenever a claim, citation, or correction comes from a third party (a commenter, a client, a DM), verify it via live search before responding — never concede or dismiss a claim on the strength of it merely being asserted. This applies symmetrically: don't assume the third party is right, and don't assume they're wrong.

## Peptides, TRT & HRT research routine
A second weekly digest, same two-part discipline as the exercise-science one above, run separately because this space moves on **regulatory** news as much as on studies, and a regulatory change (a substance added to or removed from a compounding list, a new diagnostic threshold) can make last week's accurate claim wrong overnight. From a Sports Doctor lens: this is a genuine grey zone for most peptides, so content here stays in "what the evidence and the regulatory status actually say" territory, never dosing or sourcing advice, and always points back to "get real bloodwork, see a doctor" as the actual next step, consistent with the preventive-health content.

### Bodies/registries to check every digest (non-negotiable)
- **FDA PCAC** (Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee) — Category 2 bulk drug substances list movements for peptides (BPC-157, CJC-1295, Ipamorelin, GHRP-2, etc.). This list is actively moving right now and is the single most important thing to check weekly in this routine.
- **Endocrine Society** — TRT/testosterone deficiency diagnostic guidance and position statements.
- **The Menopause Society** (formerly NAMS) — HRT/perimenopause guidance for women.
- **TGA** (Therapeutic Goods Administration, Australia) — local legal/scheduling status, since content reaches an Australian audience.
- **ISSN** — already tracked in the main routine, but flag here too if a position stand touches peptides/hormonal supplements specifically.

### Reference log (verified, citable)
- **BPC-157** — pentadecapeptide studied (almost entirely in animals) for tissue/gut/tendon healing. As of October 2026 there is still no published peer-reviewed human RCT for any indication. Removed from the FDA's 503A Category 2 bulk substances list in April 2026 over safety concerns. **Update (PCAC meeting, 23-24 July 2026):** the committee reviewed 7 peptides for the 503A Bulks List and recommended 6 — BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, Semax, and Epitalon (emideltide/DSIP was rejected, 6 yes/7 no/1 abstain). BPC-157, KPV and TB-500 passed on narrow 8-6-1 margins; the committee overruled FDA's own staff scientists, who had recommended against all seven. This is a non-binding recommendation only — FDA must still complete notice-and-comment rulemaking (historically 12-18 months), so legally compounded access is still not expected before late 2027 at the earliest. A second PCAC meeting is scheduled before end of February 2027 to review 5 more peptides (GHK-Cu, Melanotan II, Cathelicidin/LL-37, Dihexa acetate, PEG-MGF). Treat all of these as "promising preclinically, unproven in humans, not currently legal to compound" until the rulemaking actually finalizes — a PCAC recommendation is not a green light. **Update (literature, checked Oct 2026):** the human-evidence gap is starting to close, slowly. A tiny (n=2) IRB-approved IV safety pilot has been published confirming no acute safety signal at a single infused dose, and a Phase II placebo-controlled trial for hamstring strain is now registered (NCT07437547) with no results yet. Neither changes the bottom line: still zero published efficacy RCTs in humans for any indication, but "nothing registered" is no longer accurate — say "a trial is underway, no results yet" instead.
- **Tesamorelin** — the one peptide in this group with genuine FDA approval and real Phase II/III human trial data (816 patients), but approved specifically for reducing visceral fat in HIV-associated lipodystrophy. General fat-loss/anti-aging use is off-label extrapolation, not what the trials showed.
- **GHRP-2** (pralmorelin) — approved in exactly one place, Japan, and only as a one-off diagnostic test for growth hormone deficiency, not an ongoing treatment. Any fitness/anti-aging claim is unapproved extrapolation.
- **CJC-1295 & Ipamorelin** — each individually has human data showing they raise GH/IGF-1, but no published human trial has tested the specific combination as commonly used. Both were formally rejected by the FDA's PCAC in late 2024, were not part of the July 2026 7-peptide review above, and remain in Category 2 with no 503A compounding pathway as of October 2026. Ipamorelin specifically still has only one published Phase 2b human efficacy trial as of this check, and has never been evaluated in published human trials for body composition, recovery, or anti-aging outcomes, the claims it's actually marketed for.
- **TGA (Australia) peptide scheduling** — BPC-157 and TB-500 were rescheduled to Schedule 4 (prescription-only, via compounding pharmacy) in June 2024; CJC-1295, Ipamorelin, GHRP-2, GHRP-6 and AOD-9604 are also Schedule 4. On 13 April 2026 the TGA issued a safety advisory over a sharp rise in unapproved import/supply/advertising of peptide products, specifically naming BPC-157, GHK-Cu, TB-500, retatrutide and CJC-1295, and enforcement seizures since have included the "Wolverine Stack" (BPC-157 + TB-500). **Update (checked Oct 2026):** Melanotan II was reclassified in February 2026 from Schedule 4 to **Schedule 9 (prohibited substance)**, a materially stricter status than the other peptides in this group, worth calling out by name if Melanotan II ever comes up (tanning/libido claims). The TGA has made illegal peptides an official 2026 compliance priority, and a further Poisons Standard amendment consultation is scheduled for the Advisory Committee on Chemicals Scheduling in November 2026, worth checking next digest for outcomes. For an Australian audience generally: these require an actual prescription and compounding pharmacy dispensing, not DIY sourcing, regardless of US FDA status.
- **2026 Endocrine Society statement on testosterone deficiency** (already logged in the main reference log above) — diagnosis requires two separate low total-T blood tests, not symptoms alone; <300 ng/dL is a reasonable cutoff. **Refinement (Endocrine Society statement, 16 July 2026):** reaffirms this and adds specifics worth citing — tests must be early-morning and fasting (not just "two low readings" at any time of day), providers are strongly encouraged to use a CDC Hormone Standardization Program (HoST)-certified assay, since non-certified assays can read the same sample as "low" or "normal" depending on lab/method, and for men whose hypogonadism is attributable to being overweight/obese (BMI above 27 kg/m²) with no other identified cause, **weight loss should generally be tried first, before TRT**, not alongside it as an afterthought. That last point is a genuinely useful, specific talking point: the diagnosis-first message already works, but "lose the weight first if that's the actual cause" is a sharper, more actionable version of it.
- **TRT cardiovascular safety — new synthesis, strengthens the existing "get real bloodwork first" message.** Two 2026 meta-analyses of RCTs (one pooling 41 trials examining CV events and prostate cancer, one pooling 23 trials/9,280 men) found no significant increase in cardiovascular mortality, stroke, myocardial infarction, all-cause mortality, or prostate cancer of any grade with TRT in men with documented hypogonadism, but did find a significant increase in cardiac arrhythmias. Treat TRT (for properly diagnosed hypogonadism) as not carrying the major-event cardiovascular risk it's sometimes assumed to, with arrhythmia as the one real flagged signal. **The sharper point:** a separate study found men using testosterone *without* documented hypogonadism had roughly 50% higher risk of serious cardiovascular problems and nearly double the all-cause mortality risk over 10 years, compared with men properly prescribed TRT for diagnosed hypogonadism. This is the single best evidence-based argument for "get the bloodwork, get a real diagnosis, don't just start TRT because you feel tired," better than any generic appeal to caution, because it directly shows the diagnosed group doing better than the undiagnosed self-starters, not just "TRT has risks."

Squat/accessory work uses RIR since exact squat 1RM wasn't given (<180kg). Target is now closer to a 12-17% increase in 14 weeks off the corrected base (still ambitious, no longer as extreme as the original 23-27% estimate off the underestimated 220kg figure). Flagged to the client directly: deliberately overshooting prescribed loads in an accumulation week undermines the wave periodization the block depends on — going forward, prescribed loads should be followed as programmed, with recalibration (like this one) as the correct response if a load feels too light, not freelancing heavier in the moment.

## Client nutrition guide template
- Location: `templates/nutrition-guide/template.html` + `templates/nutrition-guide/render-pdf.js` in this repo.
- Reusable structure for any 1:1 client nutrition guide (not public content): dark cover page (goal headline + 3 key stats) → numbered sections — goal & approach, starting calories/macros, client's day recalibrated to their existing schedule (don't rebuild their routine, recalibrate portions/composition), budget staples, weekend/flexible-meal fix, "what's already working" (reinforce good habits like sleep/low alcohol), monitoring & timeline, closing program recommendation (1–2 matching programs from the list above, primary + alternative).
- To generate a new one: copy `template.html`, replace the client-specific content per section, then `cd templates/nutrition-guide && npm install playwright-core && node render-pdf.js template.html Output-Name.pdf` (playwright-core reuses the machine's pre-installed Chromium at `/opt/pw-browsers/chromium-1194/chrome-linux/chrome` — no browser download needed).
- Approach for the numbers: since full stats (age/height/exact TDEE) are rarely given, start from their current intake (e.g. back off an active bulk into a modest deficit for recomposition), set protein high (~2–2.2g/kg, higher end in a deficit), and frame the plan as a starting point to monitor and adjust — never present an estimate as exact.
