# WEEK 1: ENERGY

### Mon · Men's health · Carousel
**Hook (slide 1):** Tired by 3pm every day? Coffee isn't the fix.

**Caption:**
A dip after lunch is normal. Your body clock has a natural lull in the early afternoon and almost everyone feels it.

Crashing hard every single day is something else. When we see that pattern in men, the usual causes are pretty boring.

1. Sleep. Six hours on weeknights with a Sunday sleep-in adds up, and the weekend doesn't fully pay it back.
2. Lunch. A big refined-carb lunch with barely any protein tends to hit hardest mid-afternoon.
3. Caffeine timing. Drake et al. (2013) found caffeine taken 6 hours before bed still cut total sleep by more than an hour. Your 3pm coffee could be tomorrow's 3pm crash.
4. Sitting still. Six hours glued to a desk flattens anyone.
5. Something that needs a proper look. Iron, thyroid, blood sugar regulation, sleep apnoea. None of these announce themselves, and you can't diagnose them from how you feel.

Sort out the first four for two weeks. If you're still hitting a wall every afternoon, it's worth investigating properly.

### Tue · Women's health · Carousel
**Hook:** Exhausted, puffed on the stairs, always cold? Get your iron checked.

**Caption:**
Iron deficiency is one of the most common nutrient deficiencies in the world, and women of reproductive age carry the highest risk. Monthly blood loss, less red meat and a solid training load all stack up.

Here's the catch. Your iron stores can be running low long before you'd ever be called anaemic. Ferritin, the storage marker, can be depleted while your haemoglobin still looks fine. A basic blood count on its own can miss it.

Signs that usually get put down to "just being busy":
• Fatigue that sleep doesn't fix
• Getting puffed faster in workouts
• Hair shedding
• Restless legs at night
• Feeling the cold more than everyone around you

Please don't start iron tablets on a hunch. Too much iron causes its own problems. Test first, then act on the actual number.

If this sounds like you, our doctors can include a full iron study in your health assessment. Link in bio.

### Wed · Lifestyle lever · Carousel
**Hook:** The health marker most people have never tested.

**Caption:**
Mandsager et al. (2018, JAMA Network Open) looked at over 122,000 patients who'd done a treadmill test. Low cardiorespiratory fitness was associated with a risk of death comparable to, and in some comparisons greater than, smoking, diabetes and heart disease. And the benefit kept going. They found no point where being fitter stopped helping.

You feel it day to day too. When your heart and lungs are fitter, everyday life costs you less, so there's more left in the tank at 5pm.

If you're doing nothing right now, start here:
• 2 to 3 easy cardio sessions a week, 30 to 45 minutes. Brisk walking, cycling, rowing. You should still be able to hold a conversation.
• Once a week, something harder. Short efforts where talking gets tough.
• Build slowly. Twelve steady weeks will do far more for you than one heroic one.

# WEEK 2: SLEEP

### Mon · Men's health · Carousel
**Hook:** If your partner says you snore, stop laughing it off.

**Caption:**
Loud snoring. Gasping or choking at night. Waking with a headache. Feeling wrecked after a full 8 hours in bed.

Put those together and it can point to obstructive sleep apnoea. Your airway partly or fully collapses while you sleep, your oxygen dips, and your brain keeps pulling you out of deep sleep so you can breathe. You won't remember any of it. You'll just feel it the next day.

It's common in men, more so as you get older, with a larger neck, or when you're carrying extra weight around the middle. It's also badly underdiagnosed.

And it matters beyond feeling tired. Untreated sleep apnoea is linked with high blood pressure, heart disease, type 2 diabetes and a higher risk of car accidents.

Things that help either way:
• No alcohol in the 3 hours before bed. It relaxes the muscles that hold your airway open.
• Sleep on your side.
• Lose some weight around the middle. Even a modest drop can help.

The good news is it can be diagnosed, and there's plenty that can be done about it. Snoring plus daytime fatigue is worth a proper conversation with a doctor. Book a health assessment, link in bio.

### Tue · Women's health · Carousel
**Hook:** Wide awake at 3am in your 40s? There's often a reason.

**Caption:**
Sleep problems are one of the most common things women report through perimenopause, the years leading up to your final period. The average age of menopause in Australia is around 51, but the changes can start years earlier, often in your mid-40s.

Night sweats. Hot flushes. Needing to wee at 2am. Anxiety that shows up out of nowhere at 3am. Any one of those fragments sleep. Stack a few and you're running on broken sleep for months at a time.

A lot of women get told they're just stressed. Some are. But this is a real, normal life stage, and it deserves proper attention.

What helps right now:
• Keep the bedroom cool. Cooler than you think.
• Same wake time every day, weekends included.
• Try a few alcohol-free weeks. For plenty of women it's a big trigger for night sweats and 3am wake-ups.
• Awake for more than 20 minutes? Get up, do something boring in dim light, and go back to bed when you're sleepy. It's one of the core tools of CBT-I, the recommended first-line approach for insomnia.

If your sleep has changed and you don't know why, talk to a doctor who'll take it seriously. Book a health assessment, link in bio.

### Wed · Lifestyle lever · Carousel
**Hook:** Same diet. Less sleep. 55% less fat lost.

**Caption:**
In Nedeltcheva et al. (2010, Annals of Internal Medicine), overweight adults did the same calorie-reduced diet twice, for two weeks each time. Once they slept 8.5 hours a night. The other time, 5.5.

On short sleep they lost 55% less fat and 60% more lean mass. The scale moved about the same, but far more of that weight came from muscle. They were hungrier as well.

It was a small study, 10 people in a tightly controlled lab. But it fits with a lot of other research. Sleep is part of your nutrition plan whether you've planned for it or not.

The basics that actually matter:
• Give yourself 7 to 9 hours in bed
• Fixed wake time
• Dim the screens and lights an hour before bed
• Caffeine finished by early afternoon

If your food and training are dialled in and results still aren't coming, sleep is one of the first things we look at. Book a health assessment, link in bio.

# WEEK 3: BODY COMPOSITION & METABOLISM

### Mon · Men's health · Carousel
**Hook:** Grab a tape measure. This number matters more than your weight.

**Caption:**
Fat stored deep around your organs, called visceral fat, is the type most strongly linked with heart disease and type 2 diabetes. The scale can't see it. A tape measure gets you surprisingly close.

Measure around your waist, halfway between your lowest rib and the top of your hip bone, after a normal breath out.

For men, the Heart Foundation flags:
• Over 94cm: increased risk
• Over 102cm: greatly increased risk

Plenty of men sit at a "normal" weight with a waist well over 94cm. Plenty of heavier, muscular blokes sit under it. Weight on its own is a lousy scorecard.

What brings it down is unglamorous. Consistent resistance training, a modest calorie deficit, enough protein, less alcohol and better sleep. Stick with them for months and it moves.

### Tue · Women's health · Carousel
**Hook:** Same diet, same training, different body in your late 40s? Here's why.

**Caption:**
The SWAN study followed over 1,200 women for 18 years and tracked their body composition with DEXA scans (Greendale et al., 2019). About two years before the final period, the rate of fat gain doubled and lean mass started to drop. That kept going until about two years after, then levelled off.

So you're not imagining it, and it isn't a willpower problem.

What the research supports:
• Lift weights, properly and progressively. Muscle is what you're losing, so train it.
• Protein at every meal. A 2018 meta-analysis (Morton et al.) found resistance training gains kept improving up to around 1.6g of protein per kg of bodyweight a day. Most women we see are well under that.
• Keep your daily movement up. The incidental stuff outside the gym quietly drops when life gets busy.
• Protect your sleep. See last week.

### Wed · Lifestyle lever · Carousel
**Hook:** What a big night on the drinks does to your training

**Caption:**
In Parr et al. (2014), trained men did a hard session of weights and cardio, then drank a binge-level amount of alcohol. Even with a protein shake, muscle building after the session dropped by 24%. Without the protein it was 37%.

That was a big dose, around 12 standard drinks for an 85kg man over a few hours. Most people aren't doing that after every session.

Plenty are having 3 or 4 every night though, and that costs you in other ways. Alcohol suppresses REM sleep and breaks up the second half of the night. It's easy calories. And it drags the next day's food choices down with it.

You don't have to quit. Just know what it's costing you.
• Keep drinks away from your hardest training days
• Set alcohol-free nights instead of a vague "cutting back"
• Eat a proper meal with protein before you drink

If you've been trying to change your body and nothing's shifting, we look at all of it, including the habits nobody likes talking about. Book a health assessment, link in bio.

**Hook:** What's actually in a comprehensive blood panel?

**Caption:**
"My bloods were fine" usually means someone ran a handful of basics. Here's what a thorough panel can look at. Your doctor decides what's right for you based on your history and symptoms.

• Full blood count. Red cells, white cells, platelets.
• Iron studies, including ferritin. Your iron stores, not just today's circulating iron.
• Thyroid function. A common cause of fatigue and weight change.
• HbA1c and fasting glucose. How your body has been handling blood sugar over the last few months.
• Lipids. Cholesterol and triglycerides.
• Liver and kidney function.
• Vitamin D, where it's clinically indicated.
• Anything else your doctor thinks your history calls for.

The numbers are only half of it. We go through every result with you and explain what it means for you, in plain English.

# WEEK 4: STRENGTH, BONE & LONGEVITY

### Mon · Men's health · Carousel
**Hook:** Your grip strength says more about your health than you'd think.

**Caption:**
The PURE study followed almost 140,000 adults across 17 countries (Leong et al., The Lancet, 2015). Every 5kg lower grip strength was associated with a 16% higher risk of death from any cause. It also predicted cardiovascular death better than systolic blood pressure did.

Your grip isn't magic. It's a window into how much strength you've kept across your whole body. And that muscle does a lot for your health. It's where your body stores and uses most of the glucose you eat, it protects your joints, and it keeps you capable as you get older.

Muscle mass tends to decline gradually from middle age unless you give it a reason to stay. The reason is simple:
• 2 to 3 full-body strength sessions a week
• Squat, hinge, push, pull, carry
• Add weight or reps over time
• Stop a couple of reps short of failure. The 2026 ACSM position stand found that works just as well as grinding to failure.

Want an easy grip builder? Pick up heavy dumbbells and walk.

Strength is a health marker, and we treat it like one. Book a health assessment, link in bio.

### Tue · Women's health · Carousel
**Hook:** Heavy lifting after menopause. Safe? The research says yes.

**Caption:**
For years, women with low bone density were told to stick to light weights and walking. The LIFTMOR trial (Watson et al., 2018) put that to the test.

Postmenopausal women with low bone mass did two 30-minute supervised sessions a week for 8 months. Deadlifts, overhead press and back squats at around 80 to 85% of their max, plus jumping chin-ups with drop landings. Compared with a low-intensity home program, they improved bone density at the spine and hip and got a lot stronger. There was one minor injury across the whole trial.

The detail that matters: it was supervised, and technique and load were built up gradually over the first month. That's what made heavy safe.

Bone loss speeds up around menopause, so this is the stage where it counts most.

Where to start:
• Learn the main lifts with a qualified coach
• Build the load gradually over several weeks
• Two sessions a week is plenty to begin
• Add some impact if your joints are happy with it

Know your starting point first. Bone health can be part of your health assessment. Book through the link in bio.

### Wed · Lifestyle lever · Carousel
**Hook:** Training hard on 60g of protein a day? You're leaving results on the table.

**Caption:**
Muscle is built from protein, and most adults eat less than their training needs.

Morton et al. (2018) pooled 49 trials with over 1,800 people doing resistance training. Gains in muscle kept improving with protein intake up to around 1.6g per kg of bodyweight a day, and some people benefited up to about 2.2g. For an 80kg person, that's roughly 130g a day.

Getting there without living on shakes:
• 30 to 40g at each of 3 to 4 meals
• A palm-sized serve or more of meat, fish, eggs, Greek yoghurt, tofu or tempeh
• Protein first on the plate
• A shake is fine when you're short. It's for convenience. You don't need one.

Pair it with lifting. Protein on its own, without the training, doesn't build much.

If you have kidney disease or another medical condition, check with your doctor before making a big change to your protein intake.
